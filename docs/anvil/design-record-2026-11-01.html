The Anvil — the part that doesn't move

The mental side of training, added to the workout tracker rather than replacing anything in it. Readiness still answers how ready the body is for the lift. The Anvil answers a different question, and it lives on the athlete's dashboard where they see it every day.

The claim is not "we can tell you are tired". It is that Forge predicts your day from load, sleep and where you are in the block, and then three sensors check that prediction. That is why a phone is enough: a sensor only has to correlate, not to be precise. See Converge.

Sixteen tabs, all of them live. The reaction check really runs and really times your taps, so the numbers you produce here are yours. Everything else is sample data. In the app is drawn to the real components at phone width, Season test runs five college football players through a twelve game season and finds three problems, Three worlds runs football, baseball and basketball through one detector, Careers runs a league for fourteen seasons, The field scores Anvil against the published competition and it comes last, Hit rate is the piece that was missing from all of it, and Build order is what I would do first.

The placement decision

Two numbers, two questions, one screen

Readiness answers how much load the body can take today and already drives autoregulation for Free Agents. Temper answers something else, so merging them would destroy information rather than tidying it up.

They sit together because the mismatch is the useful part. Body ready with a flat head is a different session from both being flat, and neither number can say that alone. The Readiness × Temper tab is where that becomes a prescription.

Readiness is read before a lift, on the workout screen. Temper is read on the dashboard, every login. Different screens, different jobs, nothing competing.

Corrected 1 Nov · two different companies

HITE EQ and EQ Brain Performance are not the same product, and I had them merged

The daily-reaction idea is a real one with a research base behind it: a lengthened simple reaction time is an established post-concussion finding, and reaction and neuromuscular measures do track training load. The construct is sound and Forge is not inventing it.

HITE EQ (hiteeq.com), the one named as the basis for this, turns out not to be a reaction-time product at all. Reading their own pages: it measures Confidence, Composure, Competitiveness, Leadership, Discipline, Accountability, Growth Mindset, Proactive Planning through "proprietary assessments, targeted reflections" and a tool that "analyzes language, tone, and affect" from spoken answers. No games, no trial counts, no published durations, and no FDA or device claim anywhere on the pages read. The closest thing Forge has to it is Grain, not Temper.

EQ Brain Performance is a different company (Highmark Interactive) and is the one with the regulatory status: FDA 510(k) clearance in 2019 for a gamified neurological testing platform, with a post-injury assessment the vendor puts at about ten minutes. Forge is a training app with no clearance, so it may build the same measurement and may not borrow the same claims.

I had been writing as though these were one product. They are not, and the practical consequence is that the thing Forge was told to model is the attributes product, and the thing Forge actually built is a vigilance test nobody asked for a model of. Both may be right to have. Only one of them is the stated basis.

The sharper limit is in the evidence rather than the regulation. There is no independent validation that a daily tap test predicts readiness or injury risk in anybody. So The Anvil does not predict. It detects a departure from the athlete's own fortnight and says so, which is a claim the z-score genuinely supports. Everything above that line is somebody else's study to run.

It also decides who may read it. A neurological-sounding score on a thirteen-year-old is a legal surface, and this repo has had a legal surface fail quietly five times. Before any of this ships to a minor's account, the wording goes to counsel with the device question attached.

Why the flag explains itself

A pattern, never a single take

This fires on about ten sessions of spread, never on one check at an odd hour. A per take banner about the protocol is exactly what Forge pulled off the camera in September, where a banner blamed the filming angle for what was really a rep splitting bug.

Three rules it follows. It never explains a number, because blaming the clock for a reading nobody has shown is wrong is a guess. It states a fact rather than a fault. And it is fixable in one tap, with the time derived from the athlete's own most common hour rather than asked for, so the single tap is already correct.

The hour is stored on every session from day one. It is not corrected for. Fitting a circadian adjustment before there is data to fit against is how a bad constant gets in.

A state StatTile does not have

"No watch connected" is not "couldn't load"

The autonomic row shows --, and that is deliberately not StatTile's unavailable flag. That flag exists for a failed read and rewrites the label to {label}, couldn't load — under a comment making exactly the right argument, that "a zero is a reading" and an em dash says we do not know.

The screen's argument

The trend is the product; today is the smallest thing on it

One day's reading is noise by construction, which is the whole reason the flag is a z score against the athlete's own spread. So the fortnight is at the top at full width, today is one dot on the end of it, and the athlete's own normal band sits behind the line instead of a target.

Then expected against actual, the sentence no phone-only competitor can write. CARA gives countable minutes and a weekly cap, so the screen says a dip is about what Saturday explains rather than just reporting it. The claim stops being "you are tired" and becomes "you are more tired than your week accounts for" — and only the second is worth a coach's attention.

Attribution last, because "Neuro 71" is not actionable and "drive down, head fine" is: it says do technical work rather than heavy singles. The composite never appears as a mark out of 100; a neurological-sounding grade on a thirteen-year-old gets screenshotted.

Deliberately absent

No high score, and no gate

Nothing on this screen is a personal best. An athlete chasing one retakes until they like the number, stops doing it at the same hour, and treats a bad morning as something to hide — and the bad morning is the entire signal. The streak counts the check, never the score.

Bests, XP and leaderboards live in the Bellows, which is unlimited and feeds nothing.

And nothing here refuses a session. The moment a score blocks training, it gets gamed and a measurement becomes a lock — the same reason the camera never rejects a take.

Why the daily screen is four lines now

It was four hundred words, and almost none of it was an instruction. "Five is easy enough that an error means something" and "last on purpose" are RATIONALE — worth writing down, worthless to a fifteen year old at twenty to seven in the morning. The instructions are four sentences; everything else was the designer talking to himself on the athlete’s screen.

So: the four sentences live behind a ? that is always there and never in the way, and the reasoning lives here. One line stays on the screen every day — same hand, same grip, sitting down — because that one is not explanation, it is the protocol. The whole own-baseline method rests on the check being done the same way each morning, and a cue nobody ever repeats becomes drift that reads as fatigue.

The first run should be a walk-through, and that helps the DATA as well as the athlete

Four cards, one per part, each one tried rather than read, click through. Better than reading because nobody reads — and it also absorbs some of the practice effect, which is largest in the first few sittings: Highmark’s reaction subtest was still drifting 33 ms at the third. Marked as practice and never scored, so the first real check starts further along the curve instead of contributing a contaminated baseline.

After that, nothing. Your own last fortnight, the one cue, and Begin.

The column the spec was missing

Store the spread, flag on the spread

The original spec stored a rolling average and no variance. That single omission would have made the whole feature meaningless, and it is a one column fix.

Day to day reaction variability in adolescents is about the size of the deviation you want to catch. Flag on a percentage and you flag noise, every day, for everybody. The first coach who notices stops reading the screen for good.

Flag on a z score against the athlete's own spread and the threshold means something real: further from your normal than you normally get.

Try it. Tap late on purpose and watch how far you have to go before the verdict changes. That gap is the whole argument.

Changed 1 November, after the season test

The protocol is the validated one now, and the headline is the tail

The check used to be 14 trials at a 0.85 to 2.15 second gap, about 30 seconds, scored on the median. Every one of those three choices has moved.

  • 40 trials, two to five second gap, about three minutes. That is the brief psychomotor vigilance task as published, which tracks the ten minute version through both total and partial sleep deprivation. A gap under two seconds is the worse half of the old version: at a predictable short interval an athlete times the light rather than reacting to it, and the anticipation guard cannot see that.
  • The slowest tenth, not the median. A tired person mostly responds normally and then occasionally does not, so the effect lives in the tail and the middle is the part that moves least. At 40 trials that is the slowest four.
  • Lapses against 355ms, or your own 1.5x, whichever is stricter. The 500ms convention belongs to the ten minute test; the three minute form lowers it and loses no sensitivity. On a short check 500ms barely fires in a rested teenager, which made the most sensitive output invisible.

And one output that did not exist: the fade. The last third of the check against the first third. Every trial on a given morning shares the same day-level nuisance — which phone, what hour, how awake you were when you picked it up — which the season test put at roughly 60% of all measurement error, and which no number of extra trials can touch. Two halves of one check carry it equally, so the contrast cancels it. It is the same move velocity loss makes for a bar: compare the athlete with themselves twenty minutes ago rather than with themselves last Tuesday.

Research · more rules, more layers

The tasks with the most rules have the worst reliability, and it is measured

Hedge, Powell and Sumner (2018) tested seven of the classic cognitive tasks for test-retest reliability — Eriksen flanker, Stroop, stop-signal, go/no-go, Posner cueing, Navon and SNARC. Reliabilities came back between 0 and .82, "surprisingly low for most tasks given their common use."

The mechanism is the uncomfortable part. These tasks are designed to produce the same effect in everybody, which is exactly what makes them robust experiments and useless thermometers: low between-person spread is low reliability. They called it the reliability paradox. And difference scores like the Stroop cost usually come out less reliable than the raw numbers they are built from.

So "add more rules, add more layers" is not a neutral design choice with an upside. It is a move toward the family of tasks with the worst measured repeatability in the literature. A 2023 follow-up got conflict tasks to reliable individual differences with an added manipulation and under 100 trials per task — which is six minutes for one task, on its own.

Research · more colours

Every alternative you add costs time, variance and a learning curve

Hick's law: choice reaction time grows with the base-2 logarithm of the number of alternatives. More colours and more shapes make everybody slower, and they make untrained responders slower and more variable than trained ones — which on a roster means a freshman's numbers move for months because they are still learning the task, and that drift looks exactly like fatigue.

Temper's three stimuli (tap, leave, hold) are already at the useful limit. The go/no-go earns its complication because inhibition is a genuinely separate construct that fatigue moves. A fourth and fifth colour buy nothing measurable and cost a learning effect on every new athlete.

And the paradox does not bite here — measured

The fade is a difference score, and it is still the right call

The literature warns that difference scores are less reliable than their parts, and the fade I shipped this morning is exactly a difference score. So I measured it in the simulator rather than hoping.

Both are true and they are not in conflict. The first pair asks can you tell two athletes apart, and the fade is half as good, exactly as the paradox predicts. The second asks can you tell this athlete's bad day from their good one, and the fade is substantially better, because it cancels the day-level nuisance.

The Anvil never compares two athletes. It is a within-person monitor by design, so only the second question is ever asked of it — and Hedge's own argument is that low between-person spread kills correlational work while leaving the experimental effect intact. The paradox is a warning about individual-differences research. This is not that.

The cost, stated rather than buried

Fifty seconds became three minutes forty

That is a real price and it is the one thing about this change that might be wrong. Daily adherence from a seventeen year old is the hardest problem in the whole feature, and a check that takes four minutes every morning at the same hour is a different ask from one that takes fifty seconds.

If that trade turns out to be wrong, the answer is to drop one of the four parts, never to shorten the vigilance block. A thirty second reaction test is not a cheaper version of a three minute one; it is a different and unevidenced test wearing its name. Parts 3 and 4 are the ones to cut, in that order — the sequence recall sits near ceiling by design and the tapping block is last precisely because nothing it does can contaminate anything.

What is not on the table is making it busier. More colours, more shapes, more rules add strategy and learning variance, which looks like fatigue and is not. The task is deliberately the simplest thing a person can do; the go/no-go is the one complication that earns its place, because inhibition is a separate construct fatigue genuinely moves.

Why one attempt

Retakes measure a ceiling, not a state

Three things break if an athlete can retake. They will retake until they like the number, so you end up measuring their best rather than their state. Reaction time improves inside a single session from repetition alone, so attempt three is quicker for reasons that have nothing to do with being fresh. And the baseline gets poisoned, which breaks the z score that does all the work.

So one measured attempt, first attempt counts, locked after. The screen says so before they start, or the first athlete who gets blocked reads it as a bug.

Unlimited practice lives in the Bellows and feeds none of this.

Measured here, live

The web view is part of the instrument

The refresh rate and jitter in that panel were measured on your device while the drill ran. That is not decoration. This runs in a web view inside Capacitor, so touch to frame latency sits inside the measurement, and it moves with 60Hz against 120Hz, thermal state, battery saver and whatever else is running.

Tens of milliseconds, against a signal of about 250. The same order as the deviation being detected. Assume it and the numbers are unfalsifiable, because you can never separate a tired athlete from a warm phone.

So it is measured per session and shipped beside the result, the way videoAsset.measuredFrameRate and trace-sampling.ts already do for the camera. Then confidence is a number rather than a hope.

What to take away — five things, and four of them are mine
  1. The tail-10% headline. Back to the median, or lapse COUNT if the tail logic is wanted (it resolves 15 ms at 40 go trials, which the mix change delivers). Three times the sensitivity, free.
  2. The fade metric. Late third minus early third of twenty samples is a difference score on seven and seven; it never reaches 50% detection at any shift tested. Hedge’s own finding is that difference scores are often less reliable than their components, and this is that, measured.
  3. The "hold" class. Two parts in fourteen is about six trials of forty — too few to measure anything, and it carries the most complex rule in the check.
  4. The 2-of-N corroboration veto on the daily flag. Measured across all four compliance regimes: it cuts flagging by roughly 55% (3.5% to 1.2–1.8%) with no gain in separation in any regime. The arbiter should weight, not veto.
  5. The daily flag itself. Across eight cells the gap between declining and clean athletes averaged 0.101 percentage points, maximum 0.17. Keep the daily measurement — it feeds the chronic channel, which does work — and stop drawing a flag off one day.
So how is Anvil doing, plainly
  • The chronic channel is the product. 26–42% detection depending on compliance, a published floor, and it survives trades, roles and three sports.
  • The daily flag is not. It has never separated a declining athlete from a healthy one in any run, in any sport, under any compliance regime.
  • The career channel works and is correctly fenced — retrospective only, no reading under the peak, and the gate costs zero true findings.
  • The biggest threat is not the detector. Realistic compliance costs 37% of the chronic channel’s detection — the largest single effect measured anywhere in this work, larger than any design choice tested.
  • And the fourth sensor earns its place only under that compliance. Worth nothing when everyone shows up (−0.29 SE); worth +9.3 points when they do not (2.37 SE). The case for three sensors is redundancy of availability, not of information — which amends the systems argument rather than supporting it as I first wrote it.

Why a jump, and not more games

The field already settled this one

The countermovement jump is the most used neuromuscular fatigue measure in sport, and the reason is that it loads the whole chain the way training does. Tapping a screen does not.

Forge is unusual in having it already. measureCountermovement in jump-tracking.ts has read the dip and the drive off the hip since build 582, and it was built against a hip-mounted sensor rather than guessed at. So The Anvil is not four tap games plus a jump. It is a jump, with the tap games beside it telling you which kind of tired you are.

The part that matters most

The best markers carry no scale, so seven rounds of scale pain do not reach them

Every camera number in Forge is multiplied by one estimated scale factor, and getting that factor right has taken seven calibration rounds. A centimetre inherits that error. A millisecond does not.

eccentricSeconds, concentricSeconds, standingT and bottomT are clock readings off the trace. No ruler is involved, so no ruler can be wrong. And a ratio of two scaled numbers cancels the factor outright.

That is the whole argument for building the fatigue check on contraction time rather than jump height. The literature already prefers the time-domain markers on sensitivity grounds. Here they are also the only ones immune to the open problem.

Height still ships, because an athlete understands centimetres and because it is the number that says nothing is wrong. It is just not what the flag reads.

Not measured yet — say so

The ledger has twenty rows and not one is a jump

Rule #5 in this repo exists for this: measure the scatter before fitting the bias. A threshold set before that spread is known would flag noise, which is the failure Temper's z-score was designed to avoid — repeated on a new instrument.

What is known is encouraging and insufficient. Within a single set the net rises repeat tightly — 38.1, 37.2, 39.6, 39.3, 38.8 cm on one 2026-10-19 set. Within-set is not between-day, and only between-day decides this.

So the order of work is: jump rows in the ledger, a fortnight of ordinary mornings, then a threshold. Three sessions of data is not a baseline, and the screen should refuse to draw a verdict until it has one.

The resolution ceiling

A 30 Hz trace cannot see a 20 ms change

The capture runs at 120 fps, but the live analysis path only sees a frame when Vision is free, and measured frame rates have come back at 25.9, 25.2 and 39.9. At 30 Hz a contraction time is good to about ± 33 ms — and the fatigue shift being hunted is 20 to 60 ms.

That is the one real engineering risk in this whole design, and it is already instrumented: trace-sampling.ts reports effectiveHz, cadenceHeld and dropouts per take. So confidence is computed rather than hoped for, exactly as Temper does with refresh rate.

Why this needs no query engine, and therefore no budget

The Query Engine has a 50-a-day budget and a re-salted subjectCode because it returns ROWS, and rows can be differenced. This report returns no rows. It is computed server-side inside the mirror against the stable subjectId and emits only summaries, so there is nothing to difference, nothing to follow between two pulls, and no reason to ration it. An admin can pull it hourly and learn nothing about any person.

Which is the whole point: the admin needs to know whether the system works, not who it worked on, and those two questions turn out to need completely different machinery.

Averages, never peaks — and the rule that makes it checkable

A maximum is one athlete wearing an aggregate’s clothes. "The fastest 40 on the platform" is a person. "The highest readiness this week" is a person. They look like population statistics and they are single rows.

The precise test, and it is mechanical: report only a statistic that barely moves when any one athlete is removed. Take the top athlete out and the maximum becomes the second value — the difference between the two pulls is that athlete’s own number, handed over exactly. A median shifts by one position. A mean shifts by x/n. That is the whole distinction, and it is why a mean is safe and a max never is at any cohort size.

The existing precedent already gets this right, which is the thing to copy

shared/cohort-norms.ts publishes p10, p25, p50, p75, p90 and n — and nothing else. It stops at the 90th on purpose and carries no max, no min and no best. Its only min/max are ageBandBounds, which are age-band edges (14–15, 16–17) and not statistics over anybody. Every Anvil figure gets the same treatment.

"Peaks will show" — exactly, and that is the elegant part

A binned histogram tells you there is a long tail without telling you where its last point sits. You see that the platform has fast athletes; you cannot see which one is fastest or by how much. The shape carries the information and the extreme carries the identity, and they can be separated cleanly.

Three subtle leaks worth naming, because they do not look like peaks
  1. A count plus a caller-supplied threshold is differenceable. "How many above 1.90" then "how many above 1.95" isolates everyone in between, and repeated finely enough it isolates one person. Bins must be fixed and declared in the code, never passed in by whoever is pulling the report.
  2. A distribution of a per-athlete statistic has its own extreme. "Measured noise per athlete" and "where each athlete’s practice curve flattens" are both per-athlete numbers — so they get the p10–p90 treatment too, not a max.
  3. Spreads are safe, and it is worth saying why. The ± columns throughout this work are standard deviations, which are moments and move by O(1/n). Had they been ranges they would have been two athletes’ values printed as one statistic.
And a habit of mine that would have carried straight in

The simulation runs report "the worst twenty trends", "longest career 14", "the top-20 hold 23%". Those are invented athletes so nothing leaked — but every one of them is a ranked list of individuals, and it is exactly the phrasing that walks into a production report without anybody noticing the change of subject. Finding one, and it is the big one

A layoff empties the baseline, and the silence lands in the worst week

The design says a rolling 28 day window with a floor of ten observations. Three weeks out with a hamstring leaves seven observations inside that window, so Darnell Pope returned and the Anvil said nothing for nine days. Elijah Cardoso cleared the floor by one and scored off a baseline that was half detrained.

The fix is one word: baseline the last twenty captures rather than the last twenty-eight days. It survives a layoff, holds the sample size steady, scores him from his first day back, and costs nothing anywhere else — 5.8% flagged against 6.2%.

It has to carry the age of its oldest sample on the row, though. Darnell's baseline on return reached back 41 days. That is usable and it is not current, and the difference has to be visible rather than silent.

Finding two, which corrects me

Day-of-week matching was my idea and it is worse

I expected the weekly rhythm — game Saturday, heavy Tuesday — to dominate the spread and swamp the flag. Measured, it contributes only 1.06 to 1.21 times the same-weekday spread. There was very little there to win.

And matching on weekday leaves four samples to estimate a spread from, which is 41% uncertainty on the spread itself. It tripled the flag rate to 17.9%, and the extra flags are not findings: Darnell's return produced z scores of −9.17 and −4.48, which is a tiny denominator, not an athlete.

The general lesson is the one worth keeping: how many samples a baseline has matters more than which samples they are.

Finding three

Both channels agreeing is a once-a-season event

Across 447 in-season athlete-days the designed rule produced zero days where Drive and Head both flagged the same athlete. The last-20 rule produced two.

That matters because concordance — count how many sensors agree, never blend them — is the mechanism the Converge tab is built on, and I called it the cleanest answer to the composite problem. On a five man squad it fires roughly once a season. It is still the right rule for how to combine sensors; it cannot be the thing a coach looks at.

What a coach actually gets is 1.9 flags a week across five athletes, and nobody flagged at all on 72 of 97 days. For a 34 man roster that scales to about a dozen a week, which is a readable number. For five it is a mostly empty screen.

Finding four

Camp is not a rested reference, so do not use it as one

Every one of the five got faster from camp into the season: −2.2% for the quarterback, −8.7% for the receiver, on drive time. Camp carries more load than any game week, so a camp baseline describes the most fatigued block of the year.

Worth writing down because "compare against preseason to catch the slow decline" is the obvious idea and it is backwards. If a slow seasonal drift needs catching, the reference has to be a genuinely rested block, and a football season does not contain one.

A correction, and it is mine

Two of the three reasons I gave last round were wrong

I published three measurements as evidence the single-day flag is noise. Testing the fixes meant building synthetic worlds where the answer was known, and two of the three turned out to prove nothing.

  • "A scout teamer flags like a starter" is not evidence of anything. A z-score against each athlete's own spread normalises away their own load pattern by construction. Run the identical rules on a near-perfect sensor and the ratio is 0.92x — it never exceeds 1.0, however good the instrument. I was reading a property of the arithmetic as a property of the sensor.
  • "A flag lasts 1.07 days" is not evidence either. A noiseless sensor gives 1.00, because the fatigue peak after a Saturday game is itself about a day wide. Run length measures the shape of the peak, not the quality of the measurement.

The third one holds, and the one I buried turns out to be the strongest.

The conclusion survives

Concentration is the test, and the real run fails it

Noise spreads flags evenly across a roster; signal piles them on the people who are actually cooked. So the measure is what share of all flags the worst twenty athletes hold, and how much of the roster ever flags at all.

A pure-noise sensor gives 26% and 125 of 125. A near-perfect one gives 47% and 64 of 125. The real run gives 23% and 125 of 125, in all four seasons. It sits on the noise end, which is what I said — for one of the three reasons I gave rather than three.

So the acceptance test is two checks, not three: do flags concentrate, and does less than the whole roster flag. Both are free, both run on real data, and neither can be satisfied by arithmetic alone.

Changing the check

Longer helps a little. More colours and shapes makes it worse.

Decomposing the error says where the ceiling is: roughly 60% of it is day-level nuisance — which phone, what hour, how awake you were when you picked it up — shared by every trial that morning, and 40% is trial-to-trial random. More trials only attack the 40%, so going from 14 trials to 30 is real and bounded.

  • Longer: yes, to about three minutes. That is the validated short form and it is where the 40% stops paying.
  • Read the tail, not the median. The sensitive outputs of a vigilance task are lapses and the slowest tenth; the middle of the distribution is the part fatigue moves least. Free, and the code comment already says it.
  • Measure the check's own decline. First third of trials against the last third, which is exactly what velocity loss does for a bar. Both halves carry the same day-level nuisance, so the contrast cancels it — the only lever that touches the 60%.
  • More colours, more shapes, more rules: no. A harder task adds strategy and learning variance, which looks like fatigue and is not. The vigilance task is deliberately the simplest thing a person can do. The go/no-go already in Temper is the one complication that earns its place, because inhibition is a separate construct that fatigue really does move.

Making it a better game and making it a better instrument point in opposite directions, and the Bellows is where the game already lives.

What the five man test got wrong

"It does not cry wolf" was an artifact of small numbers

Five athletes gave 1.9 flags a week and three quarters of days with nobody flagged, and I read that as a system with a defensible false alarm rate. The same rules at 125 give 7.7 new names every morning, different names each day.

Nothing changed except the roster size. With five people there were not enough flags to ask whether they persisted or whether they landed on the right people, so the two questions that actually settle it could not be put. This is Rule #5 again, one level up: a quiet result on a small sample is not a quiet system.

The fix, and its limit

Flag a run, never a day

Requiring three consecutive days above 1.25 sigma instead of one day above 2.0 changes the character of the thing: 86 of 125 ever flag rather than all of them, the worst twenty hold 46% of flags rather than 23%, five of the top eight are linemen, and a coach gets 1.4 names a day instead of 7.7.

That is the right shape, and it is still weak. A starter-to-scout ratio of 1.26x is not a sensor anybody should make decisions on, and a scout team lineman still sits in the top eight. Persistence improves the rule; it cannot rescue a sensor whose noise is six times its signal.

What this does and does not prove

The noise figure is mine; the test is not

86/14 is a consequence of the numbers I chose for measurement scatter and fatigue effect size. A real phone might be quieter and a real athlete might swing harder. This does not prove the Anvil cannot work.

What it does prove is which three checks decide it, and every one is free to run on real data the day there is some:

  • How long does a flag last? One day is noise. Three or four is fatigue.
  • Does everybody flag? If the whole roster flags over a season, the threshold is reading scatter.
  • Does a scout teamer flag like a starter? If yes, the number is not about football.

Those three belong in the acceptance test beside the outcome column, and no flag should reach a coach before they pass.

Two things that held up

The baseline fix works at scale, and rehab behaves

Last twenty captures, zero blind days. Across roughly 19,000 athlete-days per season with 91 to 95 injuries and up to eight season-enders, the rule that broke on one hamstring in the five man test produced between 0 and 3 unscoreable days. The twenty-eight day window would have gone dark for every one of those injuries.

Between 1.3% and 1.9% of days ran on a baseline reaching back more than 35 days, which is exactly the population that needs the age of the oldest sample on the row.

Rehab does not flood the system. A season-ending knee in a daily rehab block flagged at 3.5% and 5.2% against 6.6% for everybody else — lower, not higher, because the athlete's own baseline absorbs the new routine within twenty captures. That is the per-athlete baseline doing precisely what it was chosen for.

Grain, the good news first

The fourteen-day cycle works, and that was not obvious

A single self-report item is worth 0.25 — near useless on its own, which is the EMA band. Aggregating a cycle's answers takes it to 0.69 in cycle one and 0.56 in cycle eight, when compliance has halved. That is a respectable instrument built out of a bad one, and it is the cadence decision doing it.

The run also detects 73% of genuine declines at 83% specificity as designed. Grain is not noise the way the single-day Anvil flag was. The problem is a different one.

The finding

Compliance halves, and a quarter of the roster never gets a score

78% of prompts answered in cycle one, 43% by cycle eight — identical to the tenth of a percent across four seasons, which is the EMA literature's prediction arriving on schedule.

The design wants 8.4 answers per attribute per cycle and gets 4.3. 27% of attribute-cycles are too thin to score, and 34 of 125 athletes never produce a usable before-and-after all season.

And it is worst exactly when it matters most: cycle eight is the end of the season, where accumulated load is highest and the data is thinnest.

What does not fix it

Every cadence change is worse, and one is catastrophic

  • One item a day is a disaster. Reliability 0.39, 25% of declines caught, 120 of 125 athletes unscoreable. Five attributes sampled one item a day cannot fill a cycle. This settles that question.
  • A 21 day cycle buys thicker cells and loses the resolution it needs — 54% caught, the worst sensitivity of any sane variant.
  • A 7 day cycle halves the cells: 57% too thin, reliability 0.46.
  • Five items a day is the only cadence change that helps, and it asks more of a population that is already walking away.

The fourteen-day cycle was right and none of the obvious dials improve it.

What does fix it, and both are already on the list

Three attributes and the content loop, together

Cutting to three self-report attributes and doubling the compliance floor takes the run from 0.59 reliability, 73% caught, 83% clean and 34 unscoreable to 0.75, 74%, 94% and 3. Better on every measure at once, and neither half is a new idea:

  • The content loop is item 12 — writing the athlete-facing sessions Grain currently promises and does not have. On its own it beats every cadence change in the table. People keep answering when answering produces something.
  • Three attributes is item 11 — the objective channel. Compete and Coachability come out of faultEvidence, completion and prescribed load, so they stop needing items at all.

That is the convergence worth keeping. The objective channel was argued for as a differentiator HITE EQ structurally cannot have. It turns out to be the thing that makes the self-report half viable, by cutting the item load from five attributes to three. The three that remain — Composure, Focus, Bounce back — are exactly the three only self-report can reach.

Careless responding, with its caveat

It triples across a season, and dropping it helps

Straight-lining runs 2.7% of answers in cycle one and 10.4% by cycles seven and eight — it grows as compliance falls, which is the same disengagement showing up two ways.

Dropping sub-one-second answers took detection from 45% to 61% at a fixed threshold. That is the real finding and it is why item 10 earns its place.

The caveat, stated rather than buried: the run models a careless answer as always taking 0.6 seconds, so "sub-1s detection catches 100% of them" is circular and means nothing. The non-circular result is the sensitivity gain. A real detector would also need the straight-lining and invariance checks, because a real careless responder is not that obliging.

Baseball, the structural finding

A rolling baseline is blind to anything slower than its own window

Ten athletes decline genuinely across the season, by 0.8 to 1.6 standard deviations. They flag on 4.2% of days. Athletes with no decline at all flag on 3.6%.

That is not a threshold that needs tuning. A baseline built from the last twenty captures moves with the athlete, so a decline slow enough to be absorbed into the baseline is a decline the system cannot, in principle, report. Football could not show this: seventeen weeks is short enough that most change is fast relative to the window.

And the deeper one, which is about the model

Forge's fatigue model has one timescale, and it is football's

The decay constant is 3.5 days. Driven by a real 184 game schedule, the simulated fatigue state climbs from 3.98 in camp to about 5.0 by April and then sits flat until the World Series. It equilibrates in a fortnight.

So the honest answer to "how do we handle constant fatigue rather than one big spike a week" is: the model does not contain it. A 3.5 day decay describes an athlete who is hammered on Saturday and recovered by Tuesday. It cannot represent a catcher's knees in August, because nothing in it accumulates over months.

That is not a baseline problem wearing a different hat. The baseline is blind to slow change; the model has no slow change in it to be blind to. Both have to be fixed and they are different fixes.

The fix, and Forge already runs it on the other side

Two timescales, which is what ACWR has been doing all along

The acute:chronic workload ratio exists precisely because one number cannot describe both "this week was hard" and "this month has been hard". Forge already computes ACWR for training load and surfaces it as a badge on the roster.

The fatigue signal needs the same shape: a fast state on something like the current 3.5 days for acute readiness, and a slow state on weeks to months for accumulation. Today's Anvil reports only the fast one, against a baseline that is also fast, which is why both halves of this run came back blind.

And the slow half is the one a baseball season is actually about.

The instructive failure

An anchored reference flags the whole roster, and it is right to

The obvious fix is a reference that does not move — compare today with early season rather than with the last twenty days. It flags 52% to 69% of athletes who are perfectly fine.

Those are not false positives. In a sport played every day, everybody really is worse in August than in April. The rule is correct and useless: it reports a fact about the calendar that no coach can act on, for most of the roster at once.

Which is the lesson: the chronic channel cannot be a flag. It has to be a trend an athlete is read against their own seasonal shape, or it says the same true thing about everyone.

What held up

Role heterogeneity needs no tuning at all

A starting pitcher works every fifth day. A catcher squats through three quarters of 162 games. A reliever goes back to back and then sits for four. A shuttle player appears in a fifth of games.

All of them produce a z with spread between 1.11 and 1.17, and flag between 3.5% and 4.3%, with no role-specific constant anywhere. The per-athlete baseline absorbs the five day rotation and the daily grind equally well.

That is worth saying because it was the thing most likely to break. It did not, and it means a baseball build needs no new tuning surface — only the second timescale.

What this run cannot tell you

I modelled the sport, not the physiology

The schedule, the roles and the workloads are baseball's. The fatigue response is still the one fitted to football — a single 3.5 day decay — because that is what Forge has. So this run demonstrates that the model plateaus; it does not measure what a real chronic curve looks like.

Nothing here says how long the slow constant should be, and nothing here should be used to pick one. That is a question for real captures across a real season, which is the outcome column again.

4. Load management is a third kind of zero

Three different things produce a light day and the channels must not read them alike: an off day (everybody), an injury (captures may stop entirely), and a healthy scratch — a star rested on the back end of a back-to-back, who still checks in, still has a normal baseline, and whose load legitimately drops to nothing. The first two exist in the other sports. The third does not, and it is the one that looks most like recovery while being a coaching decision.

Still not answered by any of the three

  • Whether the metric is real. Every world here assumes Temper measures what it is supposed to, with a known noise SD. No simulation can establish that; only a test–retest run on real athletes can.

  • Basketball’s constants. It ran defaulted and landed in the middle, so there is no evidence to fit it on — and fitting it anyway, on a simulation, is exactly the leak the registry exists to stop.

1. Prediction first, sensor as the check — which only a system with the load can do

Forge predicts the day from prescribed load, sleep and where the athlete is in the block, and the sensors check that prediction. A standalone app has nothing to predict with, so its sensor has to carry the entire claim on its own — and Hedge, Powell and Sumner tested seven classic tasks and found reliabilities between 0 and .82. The literature says a single cognitive task cannot carry a claim alone, and Forge is the only one here that does not ask it to.

It is also why a phone is enough: a sensor checking a prediction only has to correlate, where a sensor carrying the claim has to be precise.

2. Three sensors under one arbiter, not one task with a fallback

Everyone met the same reliability problem and answered it differently. Rewire added an alternative task. Outperform added an absolute cutoff. Forge added corroboration — the rule the camera work already settled: no sensor leads, none is ever switched off, and the arbiter owns none of them.

3. Three timescales, and the blindness between them is PROVEN rather than assumed

Everyone else runs one. And the reason three is not decoration is measured: a 1.93 swing single day moves a 45 day mean by 0.043 swings, and a 0.37 swing season drift moves a daily z by 0.37, invisible against a threshold of 2.0. No competitor has established that their one channel cannot see the other thing, which means none of them knows what their product is blind to.

4. A different reference per channel, each one arrived at by discarding others

Rolling own baseline for the day; a fixed anchor with the athlete’s own load regressed out — or the roster median, chosen per roster from the measured residual — for the season; the same-age cohort for the career. Four forms were built and three discarded to land on the middle one. Everybody else uses one reference for everything, and Outperform uses none at all.

5. A published detection limit per channel

Not one product in this comparison publishes an MDC, a smallest worthwhile change or a floor. Forge states one per channel in a unit a coach can read.

6. An explicit list of what it refuses to do

No prediction of a cliff. No career anchor. No career channel under the peak. No absolute cutoff. No flag on a single day. No verdict on a career. Nobody else publishes constraints, and Outperform publishes the opposite — an absolute threshold with an unverifiable citation under it.

7. And the one that is actually checkable: every choice is the residue of a disproved one

Two fatigue states became three because a pool at 95% of its ceiling after 3τ cannot carry a season. De-trending was rejected, then partly reinstated once the test world had a season in it. "Role needs no tuning" was published and then overturned. The career anchor was built and refused. The youth gate exists because somebody ran it on fifteen year olds.

A competitor’s architecture is an assertion; this one is a sediment, and the harness is committed so any of it can be re-run and contradicted.

What this does NOT mean, said plainly
  • An architecture is a hypothesis about what will work. An elegant system fed by a sensor too noisy to carry it is still a system that does not work, and that remains the open case.
  • Some of the lead is because nobody tried. First is not the same as right.
  • The simulations validate the reasoning, not the instrument. Every floor on this page is measured against a noise level that was assumed.

So the defensible sentence is: Forge has inputs nobody else has, arranged in a way nobody else has arranged them, pointed at a question nobody has answered. Three true clauses. The fourth — that it works — is sixty athletes and two cycles away, and no amount of architecture substitutes for it.

2. Do not wait for Temper. Two better sensors are already in the database.

This is the finding from reading the schema and it reframes the whole Anvil design. workoutSetEntries has 63 columns, and two of them are established fatigue markers that are currently collected, stored, and passed to a report nobody reads for this:

  • velocityLossPercent — velocity loss across a set is the within-session fatigue measure in velocity-based training, and the basis of how VBT autoregulates. It is already computed per set.
  • reactiveStrengthIndex and groundContactSeconds — Claudino’s meta-analysis points at the countermovement jump as the neuromuscular fatigue measure, and RSI is the sharper variant. Already on every box jump.

Both are objective, both are already there, and both have more literature behind them than any tap test. Anvil was designed as if Temper were the primary sensor and the app already contains two better ones. Temper still ships — it measures something these cannot, on a day with no training in it — but as the third sensor, which the build order already says.

3. The intervention already exists. Connect it rather than build one.

Everyone else closes the loop — HITE Train, Rewire’s cognitive sessions — and Forge measures and reports. But the machinery is here: shared/autoregulation.ts already turns a readiness score into a load adjustment, and Coaches Corner already has a written track called The mind in performance.

So: a low MENTAL reading gets a different dose than a low PHYSICAL one, and it routes to content that exists. For a coached athlete the dose goes to the coach, not the athlete — that is Scott’s standing rule that a coached athlete gets no AI assistance, and it applies here. Do not build a mental-training product; wire the number to the lesson.

4. The four autonomic markers: a second witness, NOT a sixth input

hrv, restingHeartRate, heartRateRecovery, respiratoryRate — collected, and the schema comment says their exclusion from the score was deliberate. That decision was right and should stand: not every athlete has a wearable, so folding them in makes two athletes’ scores incomparable.

Make them a corroborating channel instead — the arbiter pattern the camera already uses. The readiness score stays self-report and comparable; the autonomic markers agree or disagree with it. And the disagreement is the most valuable sentence in the product: "you reported a good night and your resting heart rate is up eight" is worth more than either number on its own, and no competitor can produce it because none of them holds both.

5. Pre-competition: tag the context now, score it later

teamGameDays exists and nothing uses it for this. Tagging every check-in as game day / day before / training day / rest buys an immediate win with no new instrument: the rolling baseline currently mixes game days and rest days into one distribution, which inflates its spread and blunts the daily z. Context-matching the baseline, or at minimum recording that it is mixed, costs one column.

Do not score game-day arousal yet. Arousal is famously inverted-U — too calm is as bad as too wound-up — so a linear readiness score is the wrong shape for it, and there is no outcome to validate one against. Collect, do not interpret. Nobody in the field does this, so it is an open gap rather than a deficit.

6. Grain, with borrowed wording, beside the training that develops it

Already item 41 in the build order. The addition here is the instrument: use ACSI-28 or TOPS wording rather than invented items, for the same reason as fix 1. Traits are the half of the mental side that can be evidence-based immediately, because the accepted instrument for them is a questionnaire — which is exactly HITE EQ’s structural advantage and the one that can be copied.

8. The underage plan — separate the RECORD from the READING

The vision is right and it is the best idea in this whole body of work: a continuous record from twelve to forty-five. Nobody has one. The AMS platforms lose an athlete when they leave the school, the readiness apps live on one phone, and the concussion devices keep a baseline and nothing else.

And it is the most sensitive thing a youth platform could hold. The two are reconciled by one distinction, and everything below follows from it: the record accumulates from day one; the READING — anything comparative, interpretive or predictive — unlocks later, and some of it never unlocks for the coach at all.

Why this is not caution for its own sake

The career run measured it. Pointed at fourteen to seventeen year olds the channel works — it holds its false positive rate at 9–10% from 15 through 23, because the age-cohort normalisation absorbs growth. That is the problem, not the reassurance. A fifteen year old improving 1.0 swing while his cohort improves 1.9 is 2.7 sigma and gets flagged, working precisely as designed, for developing more slowly than his peers. No threshold fixes it, because the number is correct.

5

The jump's fatigue read is contraction time, not heightDrive

Corrects an earlier version of this item

I first wrote this as "report the set average rather than the best rep", which is true — Claudino's meta-analysis found average CMJ height detects fatigue better than best-trial, and 85.4% of published work uses best anyway. But it was the wrong number to improve.

Jump height carries the camera's scale error, which is plus or minus 5% on a good day and was 28% on one box jump. The entire argument for Drive is that time-domain metrics are scale-free. Contraction time is scale-free; height is not. Averaging a scale-contaminated number more carefully does not fix its scale.

So: contraction time is the fatigue metric, averaged across the set. Height stays as the performance number and keeps the best rep, because that is what a personal best means.

What I would not build at all

Settled, and worth keeping settled

  • A ranking, on either system. Raw reaction time between two athletes mostly measures who they are. And "Marcus is a 4 on Leadership and Dani is a 2" is a sentence about a child's character sitting in a database their program can read.
  • A gate. The moment a score blocks training it gets gamed. Same reason the camera never rejects a take.
  • A high score. The bad morning is the entire signal.
  • More colours, shapes or rules on the check. Hick's law says every alternative costs time and variance, and the reliability paradox says the rule-heavy task family repeats worst. Making it a better game and a better instrument point in opposite directions.
  • A per-athlete push, until there is a hit rate. The case that earns it is not "Dani is off today" but "Dani has been off four straight days on an ordinary week".
  • Any of it on a minor's account before counsel has read it — and the nearest cleared product in this category holds an FDA 510(k) while Forge holds nothing.
